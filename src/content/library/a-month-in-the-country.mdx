Did not expect to like this as much as I did. Some scattered thoughts below

## recovery and locality
The narrator, Mr. Birkin, is recovering from his psychological wounds from the war and what he saw at the battle of Passchaendaele. He is aware he is "shell shocked" but makes a conscious effort to treat his summer in Osgodby, where he is for a modest work assigment, as a sanctuary that gives him a . He commits himself to the small town community as well as the work before him and through that commitment finds himself healing, not fully of course, but I was left with the impression that Birkin will return to life and will not be a psychological casualty of the war.

## Restraint
One other thing that jumped out to me is the restraint in this book. Conversations end just short of reaching the difficult topic. All relationships between people in this book seem to be just ever so slightly reserved. This reminds me of Remains of the Day, also a British author. As well as Kokoro Japaneese. Everyone is circling the a deeper connection, but leave it unsaid. 


## History, nostalgia, modernity
Oxgoodby was an important time in the life of the narrator, but it was also singular and insulated from the rest of his life. As it is impossible to go back to that time for Birkin it is the same for us all. As the book is told from the vantage point of the future, the narrator reminds us that many of the small town provincial charms are now gone. Looking at Osgodby (Oxgodby in book = Osgodby? Both in northern England? idk) on google maps tragically does not transport you to the 1920's. I might have booked a plane ticket otherwise.

## end
For such a small book the characters feel deep, the themes are thick, and the vibes are  immaculate. Short enough to read in an afternoon, but leaves a great impression. Cozy.
